# Data Center Backlash

Local ballot measures, utility-commission races and polling on data-center siting and the power costs it adds for residents. This page follows the politics of the build-out at the ground level, where the argument is about household electricity bills and local consent rather than about compute supply.

## Where this stands

Opposition to data centers became an electoral fact on November 3. Voters limited data-center development in 40 of 41 local ballot measures, per a CNBC tally relayed by Latin Times. The measures span 10 states, with 21 of them in Ohio. The tally carries three caveats: results are unofficial until counties certify, 10 of the 41 measures were advisory, and a yes vote meant opposite things in different measures. Georgia's Public Service Commission also flipped to a 3-2 Democratic majority after campaigns on power bills. Build American AI spent at least \$2.54M on Ohio ads, so the industry campaigned in at least one state where it then lost most measures.

The polling had pointed this way for six weeks. A Senate Intelligence Committee chairman's letter on September 25 framed opponents as a possible foreign-influence network, and the same item carried a figure that 71% of Americans oppose an AI data center in their area, a majority in every party. An ABC News, Washington Post and Ipsos poll on October 26 put opposition to new data centers at 66%. The House passed the Ratepayer Protection Act 417 to 3 on September 17, which makes cost allocation to residents a bipartisan concern rather than a partisan one.

The cost question underneath is contested, not settled. Four papers disagree on whether data centers raise electricity prices: one finds a modest decline in average retail rates, one a 2.7% rise, one a 3 to 5% rise in wholesale prices, and the fourth, a Harvard paper, could not be located. The Bureau of Labor Statistics put electricity up 4.4% over 12 months in September and attributed none of it to data centers. PJM's market monitor puts about \$29 billion of data-center-driven grid costs on 67 million ratepayers, a figure not yet checked against the monitor's own report. Three Democratic senators say seven developers resist paying for grid upgrades that exist only because of their sites, a partisan advocacy report.

Money and secrecy complicate the picture on both sides. 80% of Virginia localities signed non-disclosure agreements with developers, and the same senators' report says developers seek NDAs and tax breaks. In Texas, Gov. Greg Abbott took about \$9M from data-center-related donors since July 2025, \$2.8M of it after he paused data-center grid approvals.

## Open questions

- Do the 41 measures hold up once counties certify, and how many of the 10 advisory measures turn into binding rules?
- Does the Ratepayer Protection Act clear the Senate after its 417 to 3 House vote?
- What do state utility commissions do with cost-allocation rules now that Georgia's commission has changed hands?
- Does anyone settle the electricity-price question with data that separates data-center load from other causes?
- Does PJM's monitor publish the \$29 billion figure in its own report?
- Do developers keep spending on state-level ads, as in Ohio, or shift to negotiated community-benefit terms?

**A U.S. Senate Intelligence Committee chairman asked the Department of Justice to investigate opponents of AI datacenter buildout as a foreign-influence network, while conceding in his own letter that none of them has been charged under the relevant statute.** The load-bearing fact is the polling gap underneath the framing: 71% of Americans oppose an AI datacenter in their area, a majority in every party. ## 2026-09-22

**Datacenter operational headcount is much smaller than construction headcount, and the gap is an industry pattern rather than one operator's choice.** Reporting on September 22 puts a facility's build phase at over 1,000 workers and its steady-state operation at rarely more than 100 to 200, with engineers walking up to 30,000 steps a day across roughly one million square feet checking for burning smells and bird damage. The primary article is paywalled and this is reconstructed from secondary coverage, so treat the specific figures as unverified. It matters because the jobs case is the load-bearing argument in local siting and tax-abatement fights, and the construction number is the one that gets quoted.

## 2026-09-17

**The House passed the Ratepayer Protection Act 417 to 3, the first data-centre affordability bill to clear a chamber.** Sponsored by Kathy Castor and Gabe Evans, it directs state regulators to set standards requiring new data centres and hyperscalers drawing more than 100 megawatts to cover the cost of the generation, transmission, and infrastructure upgrades they trigger, so those costs cannot be spread across other customers in the service area. The three votes against came from Summer Lee, Delia Ramirez, and Rashida Tlaib. It now goes to the Senate. A 417-to-3 margin on anything touching artificial intelligence is the notable number: the compute build-out's cost to household electricity bills is not a partisan question in the House. ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/congress/house-passes-bill-shield-consumers-data-center-price-hikes-energy-rcna597683), [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/house-bill-data-center-energy-costs/))
